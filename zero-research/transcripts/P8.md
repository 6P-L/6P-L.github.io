# Transcript — P8 周欣然 Zhou Xinran

**W1. Tell me a bit about what you're studying for right now and what's riding on it.**

I'm a third-year student in the five-year clinical medicine program, so right now it's 病理学, 药理学 and 生理学, and the finals for all three are about two weeks apart this term. Behind all of that is the 执业医师 exam, which is the one that actually decides whether I can practise. What's riding on it is honestly my whole plan. I want a surgical residency at a top hospital in Guangzhou, and your ranking and grades matter a lot for that. But it's not only about the grades. If I learn a drug interaction wrong now, it could come back later with a real patient. My parents are both doctors, so I've heard those stories at the dinner table my whole life.

**W2. Walk me through what a typical study day looked like yesterday or the last day you studied, from when you sat down to when you stopped.**

Yesterday I got to the medical library around 8:30. First I always clear my Anki reviews. Yesterday there were about 310 due and it took maybe 50 minutes, a bit longer than usual because the pharm cards are piling up. Then I read the 人卫 药理学 textbook, the chapter on antihypertensives, and took handwritten notes in GoodNotes with my own little diagrams of the mechanisms. After lunch I had lectures until 4. In the evening I made new Anki cards from that morning's chapter, which is the slow part, about an hour and a half for maybe 40 cards. I stopped around 10:30 and did a quick group quiz with my roommates in the dorm, maybe twenty minutes, mostly shouting drug names at each other.

**W3. What tools, apps, materials or people were involved in that day?**

The 人卫 textbooks, some as paper and some as e-books in the 人卫 app. GoodNotes on my iPad for notes, and Anki for everything I need to remember. My teachers' PPTs, which they share in the class group. My roommates, for quizzing. I also used Kimi once yesterday to summarise a long review article a teacher mentioned, just to decide whether it was worth reading properly. That's it, really. I don't like adding more apps. Every new one is another place where things can go wrong.

**Q1. Think about the last time you stepped away from a subject for a few days. What happened when you came back to it? How did you figure out where you were?**

Honestly, I don't really step away from anything, because Anki doesn't let you. The closest was over National Day last year, when I went home for four days and only did reviews on my phone, no new cards. When I came back, the due count was around 600, which was painful, but it told me exactly what to do. Clear the backlog first, then open the textbook where my last GoodNotes page ended. So working out "where I was" has never really been the problem for me. The problem is the backlog being so big after a break. It took me about three days to get back to normal.

**Q2. What, if anything, keeps track of what you've already learned, what you got wrong and what's next? How well does that work?**

Anki, completely. It knows what I've learned and what I keep failing, because the leeches get tagged, and "what's next" is just whatever's due plus the next textbook chapter. It works very well for memorisation, and I trust it because I wrote every card myself from 人卫, so I know the source. What it doesn't do is tell me whether I actually understand something, like whether I can reason through a pathology mechanism instead of just recalling a phrase. For that I rely on group quizzing and past exam papers. So it's maybe 80% solved. The other 20% is understanding, and I'm not sure software fixes that.

**Q3. Tell me about the last time your study plan fell apart or you lost momentum. What caused it, and what brought you back, if anything did?**

Last semester, during finals, 生理 and 病理 overlapped with a big lab report deadline and I stopped making new cards for about a week. I kept up the reviews, but I was learning the new chapters by just reading, and that made me anxious because I know reading alone doesn't stick for me. What brought me back was really just the exam date getting close, plus my roommate sharing her 重点 list from a senior student, which helped me cut the scope down. Lack of motivation isn't my problem. It's time. When there's too much, card-making is the first thing to go, because it's the most time-expensive part.

**Q4. Describe the last time you got stuck on a concept or problem. What did you do, step by step? How did you feel about how it went?**

A couple of weeks ago it was the renin-angiotensin system and why ACE inhibitors cause a cough while ARBs don't. First I reread the section in the 人卫 textbook. Then I looked it up on 丁香园 and found a thread with a pharmacist explaining the bradykinin part. Then I asked my roommate, who'd already covered it, and we drew it out on her iPad. It took maybe 40 minutes in total. I felt fine about it, because every source was either the textbook or a professional. I did not ask Kimi about it. I've seen Kimi mix up antiarrhythmic classes before, it swapped class Ib and Ic, and since then I don't ask AI anything factual that I then have to double-check anyway, because what's the point.

**Q5. When you use AI assistants for studying, what do you actually ask them for? What happens after you get the response?**

Summaries, mostly. "Summarise this article" or "what are the main points of this long PDF", so I can decide whether it's worth my time. Sometimes I ask it to rephrase an awkward textbook sentence. I never take a fact from it straight into Anki. If something it says seems useful, I go and find it in the textbook, and if I can't find it there, it doesn't go in. So realistically I use it maybe two or three times a week, and each time it saves me a few minutes. I know some classmates use it much more, even for 病例分析, and honestly that worries me a bit.

**Q6. Walk me through how you move between your different study tools and materials in a session. What's that like?**

It's textbook, then GoodNotes, then Anki, in that order. I read in the 人卫 app or on paper, I write and draw in GoodNotes, and later I type the cards into Anki on my laptop, because typing on the iPad is slow. The annoying part is copying, like typing out a table of drug side effects that already exists in the e-book, since the 人卫 app doesn't let you copy much text. That's really where my two to three hours a week of card-making goes. The switching itself doesn't bother me. It's a routine, and my hands know it.

**Q7. What materials are you studying from, and what do you do with them digitally, if anything? How do you feel about putting them into online tools?**

The 人卫 textbooks, my teachers' PPTs, past exam papers from senior students, and my own notes. The PPTs are the complicated part. A lot of them literally say "内部资料 请勿外传" on the first slide, and I take that seriously. I don't know whether uploading them to an AI app counts as 外传, and nobody has told us. It's not only the school rules either. I don't know where that data goes. Does it train the model? Could it end up in someone else's answer? So no, I haven't uploaded teacher materials anywhere, and I won't unless the school says it's okay. My own notes I'd consider, but the 人卫 e-books are DRM-locked anyway, so I couldn't upload those even if I wanted to.

**Q8. Has anyone or anything ever checked in on your studying: a person, group, app or service? How did that go?**

My roommates, informally. We quiz each other and we notice if someone is falling behind. My mom asks on the phone, but that's more "are you sleeping enough." I once joined a 打卡 group on 小红书 for 执业医师 prep, because someone posted about it, but I left after about ten days. It felt performative, people posting pictures of their desks. Anki's due count is really my check-in, and it works because it's neutral. I don't need an app sending me encouraging messages. That would probably annoy me.

**Q9. If you think about the hardest part of preparing for this exam or course, what is it? Why is that the hardest part?**

The sheer volume, and the fact that it all has to be accurate. Pharmacology alone is hundreds of drugs with mechanisms, indications, side effects and interactions, and the exams test the small differences. Making the cards is what makes it hard, because it's slow, but I can't cut corners on it, because a wrong card is worse than no card. You end up memorising the mistake with spaced repetition. So the hardest part is really the time cost of turning the material into something reviewable while keeping it 100% correct. If there were a shortcut that I could trust, I'd take it, but I haven't seen one.

¥168 for AnkiMobile on iOS, one time, about two years ago. Some 人卫 e-books, maybe ¥200–300 in total across the semesters, since some I bought on paper instead. Making Anki cards, but only if every card shows exactly which textbook page and paragraph it came from, so I can check it in a few seconds. **Z1. [Concept: "An AI study system that works from your own materials, remembers your progress across sessions, guides you step by step rather than giving answers, and reaches out to keep you on track."] What's your honest reaction? What would make this useless to you?**

Honestly, my first reaction is caution.
